# Design Documents and Team Decisions

Establish who will read the document and what decision or work it must support. Documentation serves memory and communication; its shape should follow those needs. Respect existing project documents and source-of-truth conventions.

## Capture useful decisions

1. Inspect the user's material and distinguish settled decisions, proposals, assumptions, and unresolved questions. Preserve explicit constraints and avoid filling gaps with fabricated approvals.
2. Choose the smallest useful artifact: concept brief, mechanic specification, content table, story bible, interface flow, playtest report, or decision record. A full GDD is appropriate only when it serves the actual scope.
3. Explain intended experience alongside concrete behavior: rules, states, inputs, outputs, consequences, and examples. Define the boundary and dependencies so disciplines can work from the same interpretation.
4. When the team disagrees, restate the shared problem, compare candidate solutions against it, and identify an experiment or authorized decision owner. Keep arguments about the design's effects rather than the person who proposed it.
5. Give uncertain decisions a validation method. Link to authoritative details instead of maintaining conflicting copies. Note owner/status when known; do not invent people or deadlines.
6. Keep documents revisable as playtesting changes the design. Record the reason and consequence of a change when future work could otherwise repeat a discarded approach.

## Deliver

Produce a document another discipline can use, with enough examples to resolve ambiguity and explicit open questions. Use the project's requested format. Documentation, a schedule, and working game behavior are different deliverables; be precise about which was completed.
