# Multiplayer and Community

State the desired social experience, player relationship, group size, session pattern, and intended mix of cooperation and competition. A shared server or chat box alone does not create a community.

## Design the interaction

1. Identify why players benefit from one another: complementary abilities, shared information, coordinated timing, mutual expression, a common challenge, or enjoyable rivalry.
2. Specify roles, information, communication channels, rewards, and individual versus group consequences. Check whether one expert can take over every decision or one absent player can halt progress.
3. Model different relationship stages: strangers' first contact, repeated positive interaction, recognition, and ways to maintain a connection. Allow participation without demanding a permanent commitment.
4. Consider newcomers, regulars, and experienced players. Give each a meaningful activity and an understandable path between stages. Mentoring should benefit both sides without allowing experts to farm newcomers.
5. Inspect incentive failures: griefing, free riding, collusion, intentional stalling, exclusion, and reward theft. Identify the specific affordance and payoff, then change its design or add proportionate controls. As a modern adaptation, include recovery and reporting/blocking where the product needs them.
6. Walk a normal session and a bad-faith or dropout scenario. Consider how events, gathering spaces, identity, and shared history contribute without adding unnecessary service scope.

## Deliver

Return a social interaction loop, role/incentive table, community progression, abuse/dropout cases, and observation plan. Keep network architecture and backend integration out of a design-only request. Designing a communication feature does not authorize contacting players or publishing events.
