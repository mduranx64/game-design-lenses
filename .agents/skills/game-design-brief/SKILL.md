# Game Design Brief

Start with the intended player experience: what the player does, notices, feels, and cares about. A genre, setting, or feature list alone is not an experience. Keep the user's premise, audience, platform, scope, and constraints; label assumptions when they are missing.

## Develop the brief

1. Express the design problem as an outcome and constraints. Distinguish required constraints from habits borrowed from similar games. Use the designer's own experience as a source of hypotheses, then identify what needs player evidence.
2. Find the essential moments of the fantasy. Translate each into an action, a consequence, and feedback the player can perceive. Draw inspiration from activities outside games when it helps produce distinct solutions.
3. Define a repeated decision loop: situation -> player choice -> state change -> feedback -> next decision. Identify what becomes valuable inside this loop and why players would care about it.
4. Choose a unifying theme and inspect the four elements: mechanics, story, aesthetics, and technology/materials. Explain how each supports the experience and expose any element that works against it. Story can be minimal; technology includes physical components.
5. Describe the hook, the useful uncertainty or curiosity, and the main problem the player learns to solve. Offer genuinely different concepts only when the user asks for exploration or the concept is still open.
6. Identify the assumption most likely to invalidate the concept and the cheapest playable experiment that can challenge it.

## Deliver

Produce a compact brief with the experience promise, audience assumptions, theme, core loop, four-element alignment, boundaries, and first experiment. Include a concrete moment of play so the brief can be evaluated. Label the concept as proposed until playtests support it. Do not present feature volume or realism as proof of a better experience.
