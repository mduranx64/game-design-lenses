# Worlds and Characters

Start with the playable promise of the world. Define its rules, tensions, and opportunities for action before expanding its history. A world can support several stories without requiring transmedia plans or a large lore bible.

## Develop the world and cast

1. State a small set of world rules with consequences for gameplay. Identify what makes the setting inviting, understandable, and capable of generating new situations.
2. Decide the avatar's relationship to the player: projection, a strongly authored identity, customization, or a mixture. Preserve the user's choice; a blank avatar is not inherently superior to a defined protagonist.
3. Assign characters useful functions such as creating an obstacle, teaching a rule, expressing a consequence, providing a relationship, or changing the player's interpretation. Combine roles when doing so improves clarity without flattening the cast.
4. Give defining traits observable expression through actions, speech, and appearance. Replace labels such as 'brave' with situations that reveal what the character risks or avoids.
5. Map directed relationships: how A sees B need not equal how B sees A. Hostility/friendliness and dominance/submission can expose missing contrasts, but they are writing tools rather than psychological diagnoses.
6. Design changes caused by events or player actions and show how the player perceives them. Track consistency between behavior, relative status, motive, and mechanical role.
7. Check production scope and information delivery. Keep detailed lore only when it informs a decision, a constraint, or an intended discovery.

## Deliver

Return world rules, a role/trait/behavior table, a relationship map, and one interaction that demonstrates the design. Include change triggers and relevant state. For a small request, provide only the part of this structure needed to make the character or world playable.
