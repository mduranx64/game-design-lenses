# Player Impact and Design Purpose

Start with the user's purpose and the effects the game's actions may produce. Keep the review proportionate to the game and the requested question. Do not replace the user's creative goal with an unrelated agenda.

## Examine effects and purpose

1. State the intended transformation, if any: a learned skill, a new perspective, social connection, practice, relief, or another concrete outcome. Separate the creator's personal motivation from claims about player benefit.
2. Trace the mechanism: what players repeatedly do, what the game rewards, what habits or beliefs this could encourage, and where that effect might transfer outside play. Treat transfer as a hypothesis unless measured.
3. Consider plausible unintended effects for the actual audience and context. Focus on mechanics in the design, such as punishing absence, social pressure, distressing themes, or rewards that conflict with the stated purpose.
4. Propose changes that preserve the experience while improving player choice, understandable consequences, recovery, or access. State the tradeoff rather than declaring one design universally ethical.
5. For educational claims, distinguish successful play from demonstrated learning and transfer. Define an appropriate observation or assessment; do not invent therapeutic or psychological efficacy.
6. If the request concerns the creator's motivation, help articulate what makes the project worth doing and what scope expresses that purpose. Do not infer a hidden personal purpose for them.

## Deliver

Provide a purpose-to-mechanic-to-effect map, grounded concerns and uncertainty, design alternatives, and a way to assess the intended benefit. Keep factual health or legal questions separate and use suitable current authoritative evidence if the user actually requests them.
