# Prototype and Iterate

Turn an uncertainty into a question that a prototype can answer. Separate testing a toy-like interaction, a complete decision loop, a presentation assumption, and a technical limit. Choose the representation that preserves the property under test.

## Run a useful iteration

1. Restate the design problem and the proposed solution. Identify failure risks and rank them by consequence, uncertainty, and dependency: test a premise that could invalidate later work before its dependent features.
2. Define the decision the evidence will inform. Set an observable criterion and an interpretation for mixed results; mark numerical thresholds as provisional unless grounded in existing requirements.
3. Choose a paper, physical, spreadsheet, storyboard, digital, or technical prototype. Explain what the representation can and cannot test. A paper turn model may test choices without testing real-time control feel.
4. Scope the prototype to the question. Specify setup, rules, scenario, required feedback, and what is temporarily represented by a person or stand-in. Avoid polishing unrelated parts.
5. Produce the requested prototype or a runnable experiment plan within the authorized tools and medium. Clearly distinguish a built artifact from instructions for building it.
6. Observe the result, including surprises. Decide what to retain, revise, or discard, and state the next unresolved question. A failed hypothesis can be a successful experiment.
7. At a meaningful concept decision, check the relevant filters: designer intent, audience, experience, novelty, business constraints, technical feasibility, social goals, and actual playtesting. Mark absent evidence as unknown and irrelevant filters as not applicable.

## Deliver

Provide a risk-ranked experiment with question, representation, setup, criterion, evidence, decision, and limitations. Keep iteration bounded by the user's scope and resources. Never report simulated or imagined player behavior as a completed playtest.
