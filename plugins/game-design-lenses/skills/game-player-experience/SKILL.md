# Player Experience

Describe players through relevant behavior and context: reasons to play, prior experience, available time, social setting, access needs, and what success means to them. Treat demographic labels and player taxonomies as starting hypotheses, not predictions about an individual. The book's dated demographic generalizations are not evidence about the current audience.

## Model and diagnose

1. Separate known player observations from assumptions and unknowns. When the audience is broad, use a few meaningful behavioral segments rather than an invented average person.
2. Map each desired pleasure or need to a specific activity and payoff. Examples include mastering a pattern, discovering a secret, expressing identity, or accomplishing something with a friend. A claimed motivation must have a corresponding experience in the design.
3. Trace the player's mental model: what they believe is happening, what they know, what they are trying next, and which feedback changes that belief. Diagnose an unclear goal separately from a difficult action.
4. Examine challenge against the skills actually demanded: physical execution, reasoning, knowledge, or social coordination. Separate improvement in the player's ability from an avatar's numerical growth.
5. Look for interruptions, repetitive solved problems, opaque judgment, and mismatch between player goals and designer goals. Propose a change tied to a specific cause and describe its tradeoff for another segment.
6. Specify observations that could disconfirm the audience model. Use fresh players for first-use understanding and returning players for mastery or lasting interest.

## Deliver

Return an evidence-labeled player model, motivation-to-activity map, likely experience failures, and a focused observation plan. Do not claim a flow state from a short session, silence, or retention alone. Psychological frameworks are design lenses, not diagnoses or universal laws.
