# Pacing and Interest Curves

Choose the scale of the experience and whose experience is being examined. Interest is not the same as difficulty, speed, or enemy count. A quiet discovery can be a peak; constant intensity can become monotonous.

## Build an interest hypothesis

1. Identify the entry expectation and the first moment that demonstrates the game's promise. Mark stretches where the player waits without a meaningful decision or anticipation.
2. Lay out beats with player activity, approximate duration, intended feeling, question/anticipation, change, payoff, and opportunity for recovery. Use relative interest labels or a clearly subjective scale, not invented measurements of fun.
3. Examine three contributors: the event itself, its presentation, and how personally involved the player feels. Diagnose which contributor is weak before adding spectacle.
4. Look for contrast, rising consequences where appropriate, pauses that enable comprehension, and a payoff that resolves what was set up. Do not force every game into a dramatic climax; adapt to the promised experience.
5. Connect short cycles to longer ones. Account for nonlinear routes, retries, optional exploration, and different player speeds rather than assuming one exact timeline.
6. Compare the planned curve to player behavior and their account of memorable moments. Record segment differences and uncertainty. A designer sketch remains a prediction until supported by observations.

## Deliver

Return a beat table and, when useful, a labeled curve or diagram. Mark proposed versus observed information. Recommend concrete edits such as compressing an already-solved task, moving a reveal, or adding a decision before a payoff, along with the expected effect and test.
